01 — The Brief

Agencies don't lack tools. They lack a single place to see the whole picture.

Project Clarity was designed for agency teams managing multiple concurrent client projects — from corporate websites and game development contracts to sprint-based retainers with complex billing models. The reality of most agency PM setups is fragmented: Asana for tasks, Google Sheets for resource tracking, calendar apps for deadlines, and separate billing documents for invoices.

The brief was clear: design one workspace that handles project tracking, team resource allocation, task management, and billing visibility — without forcing users to context-switch. One screen to answer: where are we, who's working on what, and are we on track?

02 — Discovery

The bottleneck wasn't effort. It was invisible information.

Six weeks of research across two agency teams — one product agency, one game dev studio — revealed a consistent theme: project managers were highly skilled but systematically denied the information they needed to do their jobs well. Resource conflicts, billing mismatches, and deadline slippage weren't discovered in real time. They were discovered in meetings, by which point it was often too late to act without disruption.

The central insight: team members' utilization rates were invisible until someone manually pulled a report. A developer sitting at 28% capacity could have taken on another sprint's work — but no one knew until a project had already been delayed.

Every design decision in Clarity flows from this: make utilization, deadline status, and project health visible without anyone having to ask.

01
Workflow Shadowing
Observed project managers across a full work week — tracking every tool switch, every spreadsheet lookup, and every question asked in Slack that should have been answered by the PM system. Counted 47 tool-switches across one 8-hour day.
02
Resource Audit
Analysed 3 months of team allocation data. Found that 60% of sprint planning revisions happened because resource conflicts were only discovered at the assignment stage, not during initial scheduling.
03
Billing Model Mapping
Documented all three billing models in use (Fixed, Sprint/Hourly, Flexible). Each had different status indicators, different review cycles, and different risk profiles. The same dashboard needed to surface all three coherently.
04
Competitive Analysis
Evaluated Asana, Jira, Linear, and Notion for agency-specific PM needs. Found that all four excelled at task management but none offered calendar-level project-to-resource correlation without third-party integrations.
03 — Key Insights

Four things research made impossible to ignore.

1
PMs needed the answer, not the data
Every PM interviewed said variations of the same thing: "I don't want to look at a report — I want the tool to just tell me." The design problem wasn't data access; it was decision support. The dashboard needed to surface interpretations, not raw numbers — "this project is 25% complete but already 40% through its budget" rather than two separate data points.
2
Utilization is the most underserved metric in agency PM
Resource allocation tools exist, but none integrated cleanly with task assignment. The result: senior engineers were consistently overbooked while junior members sat underutilised. Making utilization percentage visible at the point of resource assignment — before the commitment is made — was the highest-value design intervention we identified.
3
The calendar view is for leaders; the kanban is for doers
We found a clear split in how the tool would be used: project leads wanted the calendar/gantt view to understand timelines and dependencies across clients; developers and designers wanted the kanban view to understand their immediate tasks. The insight — these aren't different tools, they're different lenses on the same data. A mode toggle, not a separate product.
4
Billing model visibility changes how teams prioritise work
Teams on fixed-price contracts consistently over-serviced because no one could see the remaining billing allocation in context with task completion. When billing model and remaining budget were surfaced directly alongside project status, teams naturally self-corrected — spending more time on high-value tasks and less on scope creep.
"I check four different tabs just to answer 'are we on track?' — and by the time I have the answer, something else has already gone wrong."
Project Manager, Product Agency — research interview, Week 2
04 — How Might We

Reframing pain into design opportunity.

Each research insight was translated into a HMW question that directly shaped a design principle. These aren't aspirational — they're the exact questions that drove every component decision in Clarity.

HMW Show every project's health at a glance — without making PMs run a report?
HMW Surface team utilization before a resource conflict happens, not after?
HMW Let a project lead and a developer use the same tool, in the way their role demands?
HMW Make billing model tracking automatic — so nothing lives in a spreadsheet that shouldn't?
05 — Design Process

Five phases from discovery to shipped.

01
Information Architecture
Card-sorted 80+ data types with 10 agency team members. Built a hierarchy: Project Status → Task Progress → Resource Load → Billing Position.
02
Multi-View Framework
Designed the calendar/kanban/list modes as a single data model with three presentation layers — not three separate features. Mode toggle, not a navigation.
03
Resource Module
Designed utilization rings as the primary visual language for team capacity. Real-time load visible at the moment of assignment — no separate report needed.
04
Billing Integration
Designed billing model selection into the project creation flow — Fixed, Sprint, and Flexible each triggering a different status display model throughout the interface.
05
Usability Testing
Two rounds of moderated testing with 8 project managers and 6 developers. Measured time-to-answer on three key questions: project status, team load, and billing position.
06 — The Design

Every project, every deadline — visible from one screen.

The dashboard opens in the calendar view — giving project leads a timeline overview of every active project across clients. The left sidebar groups projects into Favourites and All Projects, with live status dots (green / red / amber) so the most critical information is always peripheral, not buried.

Switching to Kanban view preserves the same sidebar navigation while transforming the main panel into task-level detail — same spatial model, different information density. Resource assignment opens the team grid with real-time utilization rings; overloaded team members surface immediately, before any commitment is confirmed.

07 — Key Design Decisions

The choices that shaped the product.

Decision 01
One tool with view modes, not three separate products.
The question we were answering
Should the calendar view, kanban view, and resource view be separate pages or a single adaptive workspace?
Why we chose a single adaptive interface
Project data doesn't change based on how you're looking at it. Switching from calendar to kanban doesn't mean switching context — it means changing density. A mode toggle preserves the navigation state, the selected project, and the team context. Three separate pages would have forced users to reconstruct their mental model every time they switched views.
Decision 02
Utilization rings at assignment, not in a separate report.
The question we were answering
Where should resource utilization data live — in a dedicated Resource view or embedded in the project assignment flow?
Why we embedded it at the point of decision
A utilization report is useful. Utilization data at the exact moment you're deciding who to assign is transformative. We put the percentage ring directly on the team member card during project assignment — so a PM sees "61% loaded" before confirming, not after a conflict has already been created. Awareness at the decision point is the only awareness that prevents the problem.
Decision 03
Billing model as a first-class attribute, not a label.
The question we were answering
Should billing model (Fixed, Sprint, Flexible) change how the project status is displayed, or just appear as a tag?
Why billing model changes the entire display logic
A "25% complete" status means very different things on a fixed-price contract versus a sprint-based engagement. We made billing model determine how progress is measured and displayed — not just what tag appears on the card. Fixed-price projects show budget burn. Sprint projects show sprint velocity. Flexible projects surface scope changes. The interface adapts to the business model.
08 — Outcome

Fewer meetings. Earlier conflicts. Full clarity.

Clarity shipped to two agency teams in Q4 2022. Measurement was conducted at 30 and 90 days post-launch, comparing tool usage patterns and team communication overhead against the pre-Clarity baseline.

The most significant result wasn't in the metrics — it was qualitative. Project managers described the dashboard as "the first tool that actually thinks the way I do." That signal — that the tool matched their mental model rather than forcing them to adapt to the tool's model — was the design goal we'd set in week one of research.

09 — Reflection
What designing a tool for tools people taught me.
On mental models
Project managers don't think in data structures — they think in relationships: "this project depends on that engineer being free, who's also needed on this other client, which has an upcoming deadline." Designing for that relational thinking meant the interface had to surface connections, not just data points. The mode toggle was the answer to that design problem: one workspace, different relational lenses.
What I'd do differently
I'd prototype the billing model display logic earlier — we spent three weeks iterating on how a fixed-price project should display differently from a sprint project, and we didn't start that work until we were in high-fidelity. The billing model question was too structural to leave until the component phase. It should have been a research question in week two, not a design problem in week ten.
On constraint as clarity
The brief was intentionally constrained: one tool, no integrations, no AI features, no automation. That constraint forced us to think hard about information hierarchy rather than feature additions. The best thing the tool does is surface what's already true — it doesn't compute anything new. That discipline — knowing what not to build — is something I carry into every project now.
Unexpected learning
The utilization ring — a single percentage in a circle on a team member's card — turned out to be the feature users mentioned most unprompted. Not the multi-view mode, not the billing integration. A number in a circle. The highest-value design intervention is often the simplest one that answers a question nobody was answering before.
