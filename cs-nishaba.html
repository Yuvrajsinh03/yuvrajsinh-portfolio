01 · The Problem

A practitioner starting her own clinic needed more than a logo.

A homeopathy practitioner was founding her own clinic and needed a brand that could hold its own against the sterility of conventional medicine — while staying true to the warmth and philosophy of homeopathic practice.


The existing name, Dr. Nishaba's Homoeo Clinic, was functional but forgettable. Nothing in it communicated the depth of the discipline, the practitioner's belief system, or the trust a patient needs to feel before handing over their health.


The ask was clear but the brief was wide: build a brand identity from the ground up — name, mark, palette, type, and the full visual language that would carry the practice forward.

02 · The Insight

Homeopathy's core principle is also a design principle.

A brand for a homeopath shouldn't look like a pharmacy. It should feel like a conversation.


Conventional healthcare branding defaults to blue, white, and clinical geometry. That palette says: sterile, precise, institutional. It works for a hospital. It's wrong for a practice whose entire philosophy is that the person — not the disease — is what you treat.


The second insight was in the source material itself: Samuel Hahnemann, who founded homeopathy, used the Greek word "Dynamis" in his own writing to describe the vital force. That word was sitting in the founder's name, waiting to be found.

The reframe
Don't brand this as a clinic.
Brand it as a philosophy with a practitioner behind it.
01
The name was the richest raw material.
Most brand projects start from scratch. This one had something to work with: a name that, if decoded correctly, contained the entire brand philosophy. The task was translation, not invention.
02
Patients choose homeopathy because they want to be seen, not processed.
Interviews with patients revealed a consistent theme: they came because conventional medicine had treated their symptoms, not them. The brand needed to signal that this is a practice that listens.
03
The practitioner IS the brand — but it can't be only her.
Naming the brand "Dr. Nishaba" risked making it feel like a sole trader's service. The introduction of "Dynamis" elevated it: now it's a philosophy that she practises, not a person offering a service. The distinction shapes how every touchpoint feels.
04
Homeopathic remedies come from plants, minerals, and living things.
The visual language had a clear direction: organic, botanical, living. Not icons or geometric abstractions — forms derived from nature, the same source as the remedies themselves.

04 · The Mark

Every element of the mark earns its place.

The four-leaf cross wasn't a visual choice made for aesthetic reasons. It was derived from the structure of the practice itself — then refined until it was precise at any size.

Petal form
Each of the four elements is drawn from botanical petal geometry — referencing the plant-derived origin of homeopathic remedies. The form is organic, not mechanical.
Four pillars
The four petals directly reference the four pillars of homeopathic philosophy: like cures like, minimum dose, single remedy, totality of symptoms.
The centre dot
The single circle at the centre is a homeopathic globule in cross-section — the delivery form of every remedy. Precise at any scale, down to favicon size.
Gradient direction
The sage gradient runs top-left to bottom-right — lighter to darker — conveying depth and movement. The form appears to be growing, not static. Dynamis.

Grounded, medicinal, calm. Sage has genuine associations with healing plants — it doesn't need to borrow trust from blue. It holds authority without coldness.

Cream, not white
White reads as clinical — the colour of examination rooms. Cream reads as approachable, handwritten, human. It's a small difference that shapes everything.

07 · Design Decisions

Three decisions that shaped the identity.

Decision 01
Rename the practice entirely — don't just redesign the logo
The tension
The client was attached to her name in the brand. Changing it felt like starting from zero. But the name was also limiting the brand's potential ceiling — it read as a local service, not a philosophy.
Why it mattered
"Nishaba Dynamis" keeps her name while elevating it with a philosophical second word. The client's name is still there — now paired with a word from the source text of her own discipline. The rename was a reframing, not an erasure.
Decision 02
Derive the mark from the practice, not from visual references
The tension
It would have been faster to search for "elegant healthcare logo" references and riff from there. Instead, I worked from first principles: what does a homeopathic remedy look like? What are the four pillars?
Why it mattered
Every element of the mark has a rationale the client can explain. When a patient asks "what does your logo mean?" — she has an answer. That conversational depth is a differentiator in a practice built on conversation.
Decision 03
Consciously avoid the blue-white clinical palette
The tension
Blue signals trust in healthcare. It's what every pharmacy, hospital, and health-tech brand uses. Using it would have made the brand instantly legible in category — and instantly forgettable. Patients who choose homeopathy are already rejecting the conventional model.
Why it mattered
Sage green, warm cream, and gold speak to the same values homeopathy does: organic, warm, considered, non-institutional. The palette is a positioning statement. It tells a patient before they read a single word: this is different.
08 · The Outcome
A complete brand identity that lives equally well on a clinic signboard, a prescription pad, a website header, and an Instagram profile. Nishaba Dynamis communicates expertise without arrogance, warmth without being unscientific, and philosophy without being abstract. It gives a young practitioner something to grow into — a name and mark that will still feel right ten years from now.

09 · Reflection

What I learned. What I'd do differently.

What worked
Starting from etymology rather than visual references was the most productive constraint. When you derive the visual identity from the meaning of the name, every decision has a rationale — and that rationale is the story the client tells for the life of the brand.
The rename decision
Pushing for a rename was the hardest conversation of the project and the most valuable outcome. A logo redesign would have made the old name look better. The rename made the entire practice legible as a philosophy. That difference is worth the discomfort of the conversation.
What I'd do differently
I would produce a simpler set of brand guidelines — optimised for the client, not the designer. A brand system that requires interpretation is a fragile one.
On brand identity for solo practitioners
A brand a practitioner believes in — and can explain — is more valuable than a brand that wins awards. The most important outcome here was that the client could talk about the mark, the name, and the colours with clarity and conviction.
