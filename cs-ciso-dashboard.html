01 — The Brief

Enterprise security data is a fire hose. CISOs need a glass of water.

Crest Data Systems builds security integrations and dashboards for enterprise clients running ServiceNow, Netskope, and Polaris environments. Their customers — Chief Information Security Officers and their teams — were drowning in alert noise.

The brief: design a CISO-level dashboard that surfaces what actually matters, enables fast triage, and gives leadership the confidence to act — not just observe. The product had to work for a CISO skimming in five minutes and a SOC analyst investigating for five hours. Same interface, two very different modes of use.

02 — Discovery

The problem wasn't missing data. It was too much, organised wrong.

Security tools don't suffer from data poverty. They suffer from information architecture poverty. Every system logs everything — but logs are not decisions. We needed to understand not just what data existed, but what mental models CISOs used to interpret it.

Eight weeks of discovery across three customer organisations revealed a consistent pattern: security leaders had developed workarounds — spreadsheets, Slack threads, email chains — to bridge the gap between what their tools showed and what they actually needed to know.

The core insight: CISOs weren't asking "what happened?" They were asking "do I need to act?" — and none of the existing tools answered that question in under a minute.

Every design decision in the dashboard flows from this: primacy of action over information.

01
Contextual Inquiry
Observed 6 security analysts and 3 CISOs in their actual working environments across Polaris and ServiceNow deployments. Recorded tool-switching behaviour, note-taking habits, and verbal expressions of confusion.
02
Cognitive Walkthrough
Mapped the mental models security leaders used to assess risk posture. Found 4 distinct interpretive frameworks — and the existing dashboard supported exactly zero of them structurally.
03
Alert Archaeology
Analysed 30 days of alert logs from two client environments. Categorised by severity, response time, and outcome. Found that 78% of "critical" alerts were routinely dismissed without investigation — indicating category inflation.
04
Competitive Benchmarking
Evaluated Splunk SIEM, Microsoft Sentinel, CrowdStrike Falcon, and Palo Alto XSOAR against the four decision frameworks. None handled the "executive view vs. analyst view" split cleanly. This became our primary differentiation.
03 — Key Insights

What the research actually said about how security decisions get made.

1
CISOs think in risk narratives, not data points
Every CISO interviewed described their mental model as a "story" — not a chart. "What is the threat, where did it come from, who was affected, what's the blast radius?" Dashboard design that breaks this narrative into disconnected widgets forces cognitive reconstruction under pressure. We needed to design for storytelling.
2
Alert fatigue is a design failure, not a user failure
When 78% of "critical" alerts are dismissed, the problem isn't the analyst — it's the categorisation system. We introduced a severity model with four tiers (Critical, High, Monitor, Noise) that reflected how experienced analysts actually triaged, not how tools were configured out of the box. This alone reduced acknowledged-but-uninvestigated alerts by 60%.
3
The "5-minute CISO view" must be a first-class feature
Leadership doesn't use dashboards the same way analysts do. CISOs check in briefly, multiple times per day. The dashboard needed a dedicated executive view that could answer "are we okay?" in a single glance — risk posture score, open criticals, trend direction. Not a simplified version; a genuinely different information architecture.
4
Cross-platform correlation was the #1 unmet need
ServiceNow shows the ticket. Netskope shows the access event. Polaris shows the endpoint state. None talked to each other. The most valuable thing the dashboard could do was show the same incident across all three platforms in a single correlated timeline. CISOs called this "finally seeing the whole room."
"I don't want to read a report. I want the dashboard to tell me in thirty seconds whether I need to make a call."
CISO, enterprise financial services — research interview, Week 3
04 — How Might We

Reframing the problem as design opportunities.

Research findings were translated into four HMW questions that became the brief for the design phase. Each question had a corresponding design principle that guided every subsequent decision.

HMW Make the risk posture immediately readable to a CISO who has thirty seconds?
HMW Reduce alert noise without hiding genuinely critical signals?
HMW Let an analyst trace a threat across ServiceNow, Netskope, and Polaris in a single view?
HMW Design one interface that serves both the 5-minute executive check-in and the 5-hour deep investigation?
05 — Design Process

Five phases from problem to production.

01
Information Architecture
Card-sorted 60 data types with 8 security professionals. Produced a new 4-tier hierarchy: Posture Score → Active Threats → Platform Events → Historical Trends.
02
Dual-Mode Framework
Designed the executive/analyst split as a mode toggle, not separate products. Same data — different information density and interaction model depending on role.
03
Alert Redesign
Rebuilt the severity taxonomy with Crest's security engineers. Four tiers grounded in actual analyst behaviour, not tool defaults. New visual language for each tier.
04
Correlation View
Designed the cross-platform timeline — the most technically complex part. Took 3 rounds of prototyping to land the right balance of density and legibility.
05
Validation
Unmoderated usability testing with 12 participants across CISO and analyst roles. Measured time-to-triage and confidence scores before shipping to production.
06 — The Dashboard

A complete security picture — at any altitude.

The final design uses a persistent sidebar for platform navigation, a top-level posture score that answers the CISO's primary question immediately, and a progressive disclosure model that expands detail on demand. Colour carries meaning at every level: green (nominal), amber (monitor), red (act now).

The correlation timeline (bottom right) was the product's defining feature. For the first time, a security event could be traced from a Netskope access anomaly through the ServiceNow ticket to the Polaris endpoint investigation in a single scrollable view.

07 — Key Design Decisions

The choices that defined the product.

Decision 01
Posture score as the hero — not alerts.
The question we were answering
Should the top of the dashboard lead with the alert count or with a synthesised risk score?
Why we chose the risk score
Alert counts are noise. A CISO seeing "247 alerts" doesn't know if that's good or catastrophic. A risk score of 84/100 with a green trend arrow answers the real question in one second. Alert detail is one click away — but the answer to "are we okay?" is always immediately visible.
Decision 02
Colour as system language, not decoration.
The question we were answering
How do we use colour in a dashboard that already lives in a dark, dense data environment?
Why we built a strict semantic system
Green always means nominal. Amber always means monitor. Red always means act. No exceptions, no decorative use of these colours anywhere in the interface. When a red element appears, it can only mean one thing. We enforced this as a strict design rule throughout the component library.
Decision 03
One interface with a mode switch, not two products.
The question we were answering
Build separate CISO and analyst views, or a single adaptive view?
Why we chose the single adaptive interface
Two products means two places to update, two codebases, two places where bugs live. More importantly: CISOs sometimes need analyst depth — especially during an active incident. The mode toggle keeps both users in the same mental model, with progressive disclosure handling the density difference.

09 — Reflection
What three years in a regulated, high-stakes environment taught me.
Design under pressure
Security tools are used under stress. An analyst investigating a live breach isn't reading your interaction model carefully — they're muscle-memorying through it. Designing for high-stress states means optimising for the seventh time someone uses the feature, not the first. Discoverability matters less than reliability.
What I'd change
The correlation timeline took three rounds of design iteration to land — and in retrospect, we should have built a low-fidelity prototype of it in Week 2 of discovery, not Week 8. The hardest design problem should be prototyped first, not last. We lost two sprints by discovering structural problems late.
On enterprise UX
Enterprise security UX isn't glamorous — the design tokens are greys and reds, the users are stressed, the stakes are existential. But the design principles are the same as consumer: respect the user's time, respect their mental model, make the right action the easy action. The environment changes. The fundamentals don't.
Unexpected learning
The feature most valued by users was the simplest to design: the risk score as a single number. CISOs said it gave them "confidence" in board conversations. What they were really saying was: the design gave them language — a way to communicate something complex in a word. That's what good data design does at its best.
