Context

Group travel planning
was completely broken.

The fastest-growing travel segment — friend groups heading out for bachelorettes, birthdays, and long-overdue reunions — had no dedicated product. Planners were running five browser tabs, a WhatsApp thread, a Google Doc, and Venmo simultaneously. One person absorbed all the stress. The rest just showed up.

Party Plan's vision was to collapse all of that chaos into a single, joyful product. A marketplace for curated group experiences, paired with a planning tool that made coordination feel social — not like project management. I was the first designer on the team with a mandate to build the full consumer product from scratch: brand to checkout, web and mobile, before the bachelorette travel season.

"Life is hard. Having fun shouldn't be. That was the brief — and it shaped every design decision from the ground up."

The market gap

The US bachelorette travel market alone crossed $5B. Group getaways were among the fastest-growing leisure categories post-2022. Yet TripAdvisor, Viator, and Airbnb Experiences were designed for solo and couple travel. Groups were a product afterthought — bulk tickets and no coordination layer. Nobody had actually designed for the group as the primary user.

Research & Discovery

Talking to planners
before touching a single pixel.

I ran 12 moderated interviews over two weeks — a mix of trip planners who'd recently organised group getaways, and attendees who'd been on those trips. I also did a competitive teardown of 8 products across three categories: experience marketplaces, group coordination tools, and travel apps.

01

The planner carries all the weight

Planning was stressful, not exciting. The product needed to give planners real leverage — information visibility without manual effort. The design had to redistribute the cognitive load.

02

Decisions need to be socialised, not dictated

Nobody could just pick an experience solo. Group chats created infinite back-and-forth. A lightweight in-product voting mechanic was the design gap every user described without naming it.

03

"Is this good for groups?" is question one

Group size, group vibe, and group pricing were the first filters — not location or solo price. Every competitor card was built around solo traveller framing. Huge signal.

04

The lead booker pays, others contribute later

One person books, the group pays them back. Forcing split-at-checkout added friction and anxiety. The checkout had to be designed for the lead booker — not an assumed group split.

"Planners wanted to feel organised — not like a travel agent. The design had to reduce visible complexity without hiding the power underneath it."

Journey mapping the five phases

I mapped the full planner arc: Dream → Decide → Book → Coordinate → Experience. Each phase had a distinct emotional signature. Dream was exciting. Decide was exhausting. Book was anxious. Coordinate was frustrating. Only Experience was the payoff everyone signed up for.

Most competitors stopped at Decide. Our opportunity was to own the full arc — giving planners a single, beautiful thread from first inspiration to the morning of the trip.

Problem Definition

The problem we
chose to solve.

How might we give group trip planners a single, beautiful surface that reduces coordination overhead, surfaces the right experiences for their group, and makes booking feel like the start of the fun — not the final hurdle?

Three distinct design surfaces. One coherent product. The marketplace (discovery + purchase), the planning layer (coordination + itinerary), and the mobile app (the on-trip companion).

Design principles I set from day one

🎉

Social before transactional

Every screen should feel like something people want to share — not a task to complete. Group experiences are inherently social; the UI should reflect that energy.

👑

The planner gets leverage, the group gets visibility

One person holds the wheel, but everyone sees the road. Permissions and views are designed around distinct roles — planner, guest of honour, attendee.

🎯

Reduce clicks to confidence, not just completion

Booking an unfamiliar experience for 12 people is a high-stakes call. Confidence signals matter as much as CTAs. We earn the click before we ask for it.

📱

Mobile-first, every time

Trip decisions happen on phones, in group chats, at 11pm on the couch. Desktop is the secondary surface. Every component was designed mobile-first and scaled up.

Design · Web Marketplace

Discovery designed for
the group — not the solo traveller.

The website was the primary acquisition and conversion surface. Users arrived emotionally primed — already inspired, already planning. The UX had to honour that inspiration mode and ride it through to checkout without a single unnecessary interruption.

1
Homepage

Lead with mood, not a search bar

Rather than opening with city filters, the homepage led with aspirational editorial imagery and a "What's your vibe?" quick-filter row — anchored by occasion types like Bachelorette, Birthday, and Guys Weekend. Users knew their occasion first. We met them there.

2
Experience Cards

Group-first information hierarchy

Every card showed group pricing prominently, a "Perfect for X–Y people" tag, a vibe label (Rowdy / Chill / Adventurous / Scenic), and social proof from real group bookers. Solo metrics were present — but secondary. A deliberate reversal from every competitor.

3
Detail Page

Confidence before the CTA

The detail page front-loaded the three group questions: Is this right for our size? What's included? Can we modify it? The sticky "Add to Trip Board" CTA only appeared after users scrolled past the confidence signals — not before. This sequencing dramatically improved add-to-board rates.

4
Checkout

Lead booker model, split-pay optional

One person, one card, one confirmation. A "Split with friends" request flow was available post-booking — not forced at checkout. This removed the anxiety of coordinating payments before committing, and simplified the primary conversion flow to its essential minimum.

Design · Mobile App

A planning tool that feels
like a group chat — not a spreadsheet.

The app was the coordination and companion layer. Where the website handled discovery and purchase, the app handled the messy social coordination before booking — and everything after it. I designed iOS and Android simultaneously using a shared design system of 40+ components, maintaining platform conventions while keeping one visual language across both.

5 core flows I designed

1

Trip Creation & Group Invite

Planners create a trip in under 60 seconds — just a name, city, and dates. Invite was designed as a shareable link (not individual email fields) — friction nearly zero. Guests joined the trip context automatically. Onboarding was a by-product of invitation, not a prerequisite to it.

2

The Shared Itinerary

A day-by-day timeline anchored by dates — but designed to feel like a photo album, not a calendar. Each day had a hero image. Events appeared as vivid cards with RSVPs visible. Closer to editorial than task list. This was intentional: the product had to feel like the trip was already happening.

3

Group Polls

The highest-engagement feature. Planners created a poll directly from any experience card — "Yacht tour or Wine tasting?" — and pushed it to the group. Native bottom sheet with swipe-to-vote interaction. Results updated live. Polls expired at a deadline the planner set, automatically surfacing the winner with a one-tap "Book this" CTA.

4

In-App Explore & Book

The marketplace was fully native — not a webview. Same group-first discovery UX as web. Key difference: in-app checkout pre-filled group size from the trip context, eliminating a full step from the purchase flow. Conversion was measurably higher than the web checkout path.

5

Trip Day Mode

On the day of an experience, the app surfaced a simplified "What's happening today" view — address, timings, tickets, and emergency contact for the provider. Designed for one-handed use at 8am when everyone's getting ready. No back-navigation needed. Everything critical above the fold.

Key Design · Shared Itinerary Screen

The product's heartbeat — making the plan feel like the beginning of the party

The itinerary was the most-opened screen in the app. I designed it around one question: "What does this need to feel like?" The answer was: a hype reel, not a to-do list. Editorial imagery on every day card, RSVP dots showing who's in, and empty days that acted as prompts — "Nothing yet · Explore Nashville →" — turning dead space into conversion surface.

A

Participant RSVP dots: Each event card showed avatar icons of who's attending. Social accountability baked in — a subtle, friendly nudge to confirm.

B

Planner reveal mode: A toggle let the planner hide itinerary events until they were ready — preserving the surprise element for guests before the trip.

C

Empty states as hooks: Blank days showed city-specific experience suggestions inline — turning gaps in the itinerary into natural discovery moments.

Key Design · Group Poll Screen

Decision-making made social — and fast

The poll flow took four steps: experience card → poll created → shared to group → results in. Designed with swipe-to-vote (left to skip, right to vote) and a live percentage bar updating in real time. Polls expired at the planner's deadline — with a winner auto-surfaced and a "Book this" CTA ready to go.

A

Rich option cards: Each poll option showed the experience image, name, price/person, and a one-line description — enough to decide without leaving the poll screen.

B

Personalised nudge notification: "Hey Sarah, 3 people voted — your turn!" — sent 4 hours before deadline. Drove a 78% poll completion rate.

Reflection

What I learned building
consumer at scale.

This was my first fully consumer-facing product designed end-to-end. The shift from enterprise B2B UX to consumer brought lessons I couldn't have predicted.

💃

Consumer users don't read interfaces — they feel them. The first homepage was well-organized but emotionally flat. Redesign started with a single question: what does it feel like to book a bachelorette trip? Once I answered that, every layout decision became obvious.

🧪

Research before instinct

Every assumption I brought from B2B UX — rational flows, feature density, admin-first hierarchy — was wrong for this audience. The research phase wasn't project setup. It was deprogramming. I'm a better designer for every consumer interview I ran.

🔁

Virality is a design decision

The group poll mechanic wasn't a growth hack — it was the right UX for the problem. When design solves the real coordination problem, sharing happens naturally. You don't need to bolt it on later. The 4× growth loop came from solving the actual pain.

📱

Mobile isn't a screen — it's a moment

The day-of mode taught me that mobile design isn't just small screens. It's a specific time, place, and emotional state — 8am, one hand, everyone's rushing. Every tap counts. Designing for the moment, not the device, changed how I think about mobile entirely.

"The best design decision on this project wasn't a screen — it was choosing not to build a group chat. We got out of the way and let people use WhatsApp, where they already were."

Knowing what not to build is the hardest skill in product design. Users weren't looking for another inbox — they needed a planning layer that complemented their existing communication, not replaced it. Research gave us that answer before we got attached to the idea. That's the lesson I'd carry into any consumer product: talk to people first, build second.
