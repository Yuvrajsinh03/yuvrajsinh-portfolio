The Brief

Group travel had a coordination problem. Nobody had designed for the group as the primary user.

A consumer travel startup was building a group experiences marketplace — bachelorette weekends, birthday trips, girls' getaways. They had supply: 3,000+ curated experiences across 50+ cities. What they didn't have was a product that actually solved the group planning problem.


Existing platforms — Viator, Airbnb Experiences, GetYourGuide — were built for one person deciding for themselves. Groups were an afterthought: no size filters, no shared decision tools, no coordination layer. The back-and-forth happened in WhatsApp, and it was exhausting.


I was brought in as the sole designer to design the full product — web marketplace and iOS/Android app — from a blank Figma file.

01 · Discovery

I spent three weeks watching how real friend groups plan trips. It was messier than I expected.

Before designing a single screen, I needed to understand the actual group planning process — not the idealised version, but the real one: the WhatsApp threads, the poll screenshots, the friend who never responds. The research was qualitative and deliberately messy.

01
Diary Studies
Asked 12 women to document a real group trip they were planning over 3 weeks. Every tool they used, every decision point, every frustration — logged via Loom video and voice notes. Raw material, not recall.
02
Group Interview Sessions
Ran 5 sessions with full friend groups (not individuals). Watched how they negotiated decisions together. Who deferred. Who drove. Who vetoed. Group dynamics change everything about how a decision actually gets made.
03
Competitive Audit
Booked experiences on Airbnb, Viator, GetYourGuide, and ClassPass for a group of 6 as a group. Documented every friction point. The drop-offs, the workarounds, the moments we had to leave the product to continue planning.
04
WhatsApp Thread Analysis
With consent, analysed 8 real planning threads. Categorised every message type: suggestions, questions, votes, confirmations, confusion. The patterns revealed which decisions needed in-product support and which didn't.

02 · Synthesis

Four truths that changed what I was designing.

The diary studies and group sessions produced over 200 observations. After affinity mapping across two sessions, four insights kept coming up — and each one had direct consequences for the product.

01
There's always one person doing 80% of the planning work.
Every group has an organiser. They carry the cognitive load — researching options, chasing responses, making the booking. Existing products gave them no support and no status. Designing for the organiser as a first-class user, not a generic visitor, was the first design unlock.
02
Group decisions stall not because of disagreement, but because of silence.
Most planning threads didn't fail because friends had conflicting preferences — they stalled because not everyone responded. One non-answer could freeze a decision for days. The product needed a coordination mechanic that surfaced consensus without requiring everyone to actively engage at the same moment.
03
The biggest purchase question is always: "will this work for our group size?"
Every experience listing I tested buried group size compatibility. Users had to scroll or click into a detail page to find out if an experience even accepted their group. This was the single biggest source of drop-off — users who left to "check if it works for 9 people" rarely came back.
04
The "day of the trip" experience was completely undesigned territory.
Every competitor focused on pre-trip: browse, book, done. But users described the day of the experience as a second planning crisis — finding the venue, sharing tickets, managing the group arriving at different times. Nobody had designed for this moment. There was a product gap at the very end of the journey.
"I end up doing everything. I find it, I share it, I chase everyone for money, I make the booking. By the time we actually go, I'm exhausted."
— Trip organiser, diary study participant, planning a bachelorette for 11 people

03 · Define

Turning research into design opportunities.

With four clear insights, I reframed the problem as a set of "How Might We" questions. These became the brief every feature had to pass before I committed pixels to it.

HMW make the organiser feel supported and visible — not just another anonymous user — so they stay motivated through the full planning journey?
HMW collect group opinions without requiring everyone to be online at the same time — and surface consensus even when not everyone responds?
HMW surface group-size compatibility before the user clicks into an experience — so they never waste time on something that won't work for their group?
HMW design for the day of the trip — when people are nervous, rushed, and looking at their phone — as a distinct product moment?
HMW let the group booking flow feel like a shared experience — not one person filling a form on behalf of everyone else?

04 · Ideation

The concepts I explored — and the ones that didn't survive testing.

I ran two ideation rounds. The first was divergent — no constraints, 40+ concepts across web and mobile, including some that were clearly too ambitious for a Series A product team of 4 engineers.


The second round was convergent: every concept had to solve a specific research insight, work within the existing tech stack, and be testable with a prototype in a week. That constraint killed a lot of things that felt exciting but weren't grounded in a real user problem.


I tested 6 core concepts via Maze with 28 participants across two rounds. The group poll mechanic emerged as the most viral and most usable — users started sharing poll links with their friends before we'd even added it to the product brief.

04b · Product Flow

The redesigned group planning journey.

The product flow mapped onto five distinct moments — each with a different emotional state, different device context, and different design challenge.

01
Discover
Group size + vibe filters surface the right experiences instantly. No dead ends from capacity mismatches. Size compatibility shown in the card.
02
Group Poll
Organiser pins 2–4 experiences, shares a poll link. Friends vote async — no account needed. Consensus surfaces automatically when a clear winner emerges.
03
Detail + Book
Group size pre-filled from poll data. Three questions front-loaded: group size, dietary needs, accessibility. No redundant form-filling.
04
Pre-Trip
Countdown, confirmation details, and share-to-group summary. All the context the organiser needs to brief the group without leaving the app.
05
Day-Of Mode
Address, time, tickets, emergency contact — all above the fold. One-handed, 8am, everyone's rushing. Designed for the moment, not the screen.

05 · Design Decisions

Four decisions that shaped the product.

There were hundreds of decisions across web and mobile. These four had the most debate, the most testing iterations, and the most measurable downstream effect.

Decision 01
Group size as the primary filter — not category or price
The tension
The initial product spec had category (spa, outdoor, food) as the top-level filter — standard marketplace logic. But research showed group size was the first disqualifier. Users didn't care if an experience was "outdoor" if it only accepted 6 people and their group was 11. We were filtering by the wrong dimension first.
Why it mattered
Making group size the lead filter reduced catalogue overwhelm and eliminated the dead-end pattern (click → love it → doesn't fit group → back). It also gave us a data signal we hadn't had before: groups told us their size upfront, which pre-filled everything downstream — poll, booking form, capacity check.
Decision 02
No account required to vote in a group poll
The tension
Engineering wanted account-gated voting — easier to track, less anonymous, cleaner data model. Product instinct said friction kills participation. If voting required sign-up, the organiser would be chasing friends to create accounts instead of getting actual votes. The whole mechanic would collapse.
Why it mattered
Anonymous link voting meant a poll link shared in WhatsApp could collect 8 votes in 20 minutes — no app download, no sign-up. The acquisition loop was built into the mechanic itself: friends who voted saw the full app and often returned to browse independently. Virality came from the right design decision, not a growth hack.
Decision 03
The "Add to Trip Board" CTA appeared after the confidence signals, not before
The tension
Standard e-commerce convention: CTA above the fold. PMs wanted it prominent and early. But user research was clear — group buyers had three questions before they'd consider booking: Is this right for our size? What's included? Can we modify it? Adding to a trip board before those were answered felt premature.
Why it mattered
The detail page front-loaded the three confidence questions in a structured accordion. "Add to Trip Board" appeared sticky at the bottom only after users scrolled past those answers — not before. In Maze testing, add-to-board rates went from 34% to 61% when we made this change. Sequencing the CTA after confidence rather than before anxiety was the difference.
Decision 04
Day-Of mode as a distinct app state — not a deep-linked screen
The tension
Engineering proposed a deep link to the booking confirmation page for "day-of" use. It worked technically. But confirmation pages are designed for post-purchase reassurance, not for one-handed use at 8am when everyone's getting ready. They had too much information and the wrong information hierarchy for the actual moment.
Why it mattered
Day-Of mode was a separate app state — triggered automatically on the morning of a booked experience. It showed: address (with one-tap Maps open), start time, ticket QR, emergency contact for the provider, and a single "Share with Group" button. Five pieces of information, ruthlessly prioritised. Everything above the fold. Session data showed 74% of groups used it on experience day.

08 · Reflection

What I learned building consumer at scale.

What worked
The decision to prioritise group size as the primary filter was the most impactful single design move on the project. It reframed every subsequent decision — booking flow, poll mechanic, capacity logic — because it was grounded in the real first question users had, not the conventional marketplace hierarchy. When you get the first filter right, everything else gets easier.
What I'd do differently
I'd involve the "reluctant friend" archetype earlier — the person in every group who doesn't engage with planning but whose eventual sign-off is required. I designed primarily for the organiser and the engaged participant. The passive member was an afterthought whose behaviour pattern I only understood fully after the poll mechanic launched and we saw how rarely they voted.
The uncomfortable truth
The best design decision on this project wasn't a screen. It was choosing not to build a group chat. We got out of the way and let people use WhatsApp, where they already were. Knowing what not to build is the hardest skill in product design — and the most valuable. Research gave us that answer before we got attached to the idea.
Domain lesson
Consumer users don't read interfaces — they feel them. The shift from B2B UX to consumer was a genuine deprogramming. In enterprise design, the UI conveys information. In consumer design, it conveys mood. Delight isn't a nice-to-have in the group travel category — it's a product requirement. The first screen sets the emotional expectation for the entire trip.
