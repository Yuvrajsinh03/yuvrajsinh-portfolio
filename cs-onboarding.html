The Brief

Eight tools. No shared context. Analysts were spending more time chasing evidence than making decisions.

A regtech startup was closing the gap between detecting financial crime and resolving it. The detection side had grown smarter — but the operational side hadn't. Eight disconnected tools, no shared context. Every investigation started from scratch.


Every morning, analysts opened multiple tabs — the platform, a sanctions list, a risk policy doc, a team chat, a spreadsheet. Alerts landed with no context. RFIs were managed through email chains. The tools had been built for the audit, not for the person doing the work.


Each client's regulatory policy defined a different set of compliance jobs — from document collection and validation to entity screening, UBO verification and risk analysis — all done by hand, across separate systems. I was brought in to redesign the core review experience — the part analysts spend 6–8 hours inside every day — as a single workbook where AI agents run the compliance jobs and the analyst reviews, annotates and decides.

01 · Discovery

I spent two weeks not designing anything. Just watching.

Before touching Figma, I needed to understand the actual work — what decisions analysts make, in what order, with what information, and where they fail. Just as important: which parts were judgement, and which were simply labour an agent could take on. The first day told me a lot: alerts landed with no context, RFIs were managed through email chains, and analysts were spending more time chasing context than resolving cases. Desk research on KYC regulation was table stakes. The real insight came from sitting next to analysts while they worked.

01
Contextual Inquiry
Shadowed 6 KYC analysts across 3 client organisations over 8 working days. Watched how they navigated the existing tool, what they ignored, where they paused, what made them curse under their breath.
02
Stakeholder Interviews
14 interviews — 8 analysts, 3 compliance managers, 2 team leads, 1 auditor. The auditor interview was the most revealing: I needed to understand what evidence they'd look for if something went wrong.
03
Workflow Audit
Mapped every step of the existing review workflow — from case assignment to final decision — including all the workarounds analysts had invented. Each workaround was a signal of an unmet need.
04
Regulatory Research
Read the relevant FATF guidelines, PML Act requirements, and client-specific compliance policies. Designers who build compliance products without reading the regulation are guessing. I didn't want to guess.

02 · Synthesis

Five things that changed everything.

The research produced 60+ observations. After affinity mapping and two rounds of synthesis, one pattern kept emerging: every tool in the ecosystem had been built for the regulator — nothing was built for the analyst doing the daily work. These five insights made that concrete, and each had direct consequences for how analysts and agents should share the work.

01
Every investigation started from scratch.
Eight disconnected tools. No shared context between them. An analyst opening a new case had to rebuild the picture every time — pulling sanctions data from one tool, identity documents from another, risk policies from a third. The gap between detecting a risk and resolving it was where all the cost lived.
02
The tools were built for the regulator, not the analyst.
Every compliance product in the ecosystem was designed to satisfy audit requirements — checkbox fields, mandatory form submissions, structured exports. None of it was built for the person doing the daily work. Analysts were spending more time feeding the system than resolving cases.
03
Each client's compliance process was different.
Onboarding steps varied by client. Risk scoring fundamentals differed based on each organisation's CDD and EDD methods. A one-size-fits-all workflow couldn't serve three clients, let alone thirty. The jobs the system needed to run had to come from each client's own regulatory policy document.
04
The audit trail was retrospective, not concurrent.
Analysts completed a review, then logged their reasoning separately. Reasoning was reconstructed post-hoc, not captured in the moment. The audit trail was technically present but evidentially weak — a quarterly scramble to prove decisions had been made properly. Auditors had flagged this repeatedly.
05
Automation without transparency would make it worse.
The instinct was to automate everything. But after 40 cases, pattern recognition kicks in and anomalies get glossed over. Add an AI agent that looks reliable, and the risk grows: the more the agent handles, the less the analyst questions. Any automation had to keep the human visibly in the loop — not just in control, but able to see exactly what the agent did and why.

03 · Define

Reframing the problem as opportunity.

Once I had the insights, I needed design opportunities — not solutions. These HMW statements became the filter every design decision was tested against throughout the project.

HMW give analysts a clear view of what needs their attention the moment they start their day — without opening eight different tools?
HMW close the gap between detecting a risk and resolving it — in one workspace instead of across fragmented systems?
HMW let each client's compliance policy define the jobs — so the system adapts to the regulation, not the other way around?
HMW make the audit trail a byproduct of good workflow — evidence by default, not an additional task the analyst writes after the fact?
HMW let AI agents handle the repetitive compliance jobs while the analyst always sees what the agent did — and stays in control of the decision?

04 · From opportunity to structure

Each HMW became a focused ideation anchor. I tested lo-fi prototypes with 4 analysts across 45-minute sessions — watching for hesitation as much as feedback. Two concepts were set aside early. Three became the foundation of the redesign.

× Separate dashboards per compliance stage — fragmented the analyst's view further
× Fully autonomous agent decisions — removed analyst oversight and broke the trust model
✓ Policy-driven job generation (workbook adapts to each client's regulatory requirements)
✓ Unified context graph (all entities, signals and history linked in one workspace)
✓ Inline decision annotation with agent transparency (every agent action visible, reasoning captured mid-flow)

04b · Design Flow

The redesigned workflow: agents run, analysts decide.

The new workflow reduced the number of decision points per case from 7 to 4, without removing any compliance-required checks. Fewer steps, same regulatory rigour.

01
Day View
Analysts start their day with a clear view of what jobs need attention — cases surfaced by risk tier, high-risk first. No more opening eight tabs to figure out where to begin.
02
Agents Run
Each client's policy document generates the jobs — e.g. Document Collection & Validation, Director & UBO Verification, Entity Screening, Risk Analysis. AI agents run each job with full transparency: live status, every action logged. Decisions always stay with the analyst.
03
Review + Annotate
Agent findings sit next to the evidence. Inline annotation tied to each section; reasoning captured as the analyst reviews — not after. Completeness indicator live.
04
Decision
Approve, reject, or escalate — with a required reason code. Soft warning if data completeness is below threshold. A signal, not a blocker.

05 · Design Decisions

Four decisions that defined the product.

Every design has a hundred small decisions. These four had the most argument, the most testing, and the most impact.

Decision 01
One workspace, not eight tools
The tension
The existing workflow spread compliance work across eight disconnected tools — each built for a different stage. Integrating them into a single view was technically possible but risked information overload. The question wasn't whether to unify — it was how much to show at once.
Why it mattered
The workbook became the single workspace — all entities, signals and history linked in one context graph. But instead of showing everything at once, each job surfaces its own findings progressively. The analyst sees what matters for the job in front of them, with the full picture one click deeper.
Decision 02
Policy-driven jobs, not a fixed workflow
The tension
Engineering wanted a standard set of steps every case would follow. But each client's regulatory policy defined different CDD and EDD methods — different onboarding steps, different risk scoring fundamentals. A one-size-fits-all workflow couldn't serve three clients, let alone thirty.
Why it mattered
The workbook generates its jobs from each client's policy document. The number and type of jobs vary — Document Collection, UBO Verification, Entity Screening, Risk Analysis are examples, not a fixed list. This made the system adaptable without requiring a redesign for every new client.
Decision 03
Agents run, analysts decide
The tension
There was pressure to let agents make decisions autonomously — flag, clear, escalate — to maximise throughput. But the more reliable the agent looks, the less the analyst questions it. Full autonomy would have removed the human judgement that makes compliance decisions defensible.
Why it mattered
Agents run the jobs — collecting documents, verifying identities, screening entities — but every decision stays with the analyst. The agent's work is fully transparent: live status, every action logged, findings presented alongside evidence. The analyst can stop an agent, add their own notes, and always sees what was automated versus what requires their judgement.
Decision 04
Evidence by default, not quarterly scramble
The tension
The existing system required analysts to write a review summary after each case. It was done badly or not at all — and come audit season, teams scrambled to reconstruct evidence. The audit trail had to exist. It just couldn't feel like admin.
Why it mattered
Every annotation made during review — each flag, each comment, each reason code — and each action an agent took is automatically compiled into the audit log. A clear line between what the agent did and what the human decided. By the time the analyst submits, the trail is already written. Evidence by default, not evidence by effort.

06 · The Design

What shipped.

The redesigned platform shipped in phases — the triage view and split-pane layout first, followed by the context sidebar and decision annotation in the second release. Throughout, the agents' work is visible rather than hidden: the analyst always knows what is running, what it found and what it still needs.

Overview — Customer Summary
Risk-tiered case queue. Colour, typography weight, and positioning all signal priority. No numerical scores visible at the list level.
Agent - Running State
The AI agent is collecting documents with preloaded configurations. Status stays visible while the analyst works on other parts of the case.
Screening Results
Results from third-party screening tools are pulled into the workbook and summarised in one place — no separate tab to open.
Workbook walkthrough
Inline annotations captured mid-review. Completeness indicator visible before submission. Soft warning triggers if reasoning is absent.

08 · Reflection

What I learned. What I'd do differently.

What worked
The decision to frame the audit trail as a workflow output rather than a task was the single most impactful design move on the project. It closed the gap between detection and resolution — the operational gap the whole platform was built to eliminate. Automate the labour, never the accountability. That principle shaped every agent-human boundary in the product.
What I'd do differently
I would involve the compliance auditor earlier — ideally in the research phase. I only interviewed one auditor, late in discovery. Their perspective on what "good evidence" looks like would have shaped some decisions I made by assumption rather than research.
The uncomfortable truth
The biggest design challenge was not the interface. It was analyst fatigue — a structural, organisational problem that no UI can fix. The redesign improved the tool. It didn't solve the problem of asking people to make high-stakes decisions 60+ times a day. I made that observation in my handoff documentation.
Domain lesson
In regulated industries, design is evidence. Every default, every warning, every confirmation becomes part of the compliance record. The existing tools had been built for the auditor reviewing afterwards — not for the analyst doing the work. When an AI agent contributes to a decision, the interface has to make clear what the agent did and what a person decided. That distinction is not a feature — it's the entire trust model.
