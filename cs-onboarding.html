The Brief

The compliance review interface was breaking the people meant to use it.

A regtech startup had built a customer onboarding platform used by KYC/AML compliance teams at financial institutions. The platform handled the full onboarding submission pipeline — document intake, risk scoring, analyst review, and approval or rejection.


The product worked. But the experience of using it daily was exhausting analysts. Error rates were rising. Review throughput was falling. And the compliance managers knew an audit was coming.


I was brought in to redesign the core review interface — the part analysts spend 6–8 hours inside every day.

01 · Discovery

I spent two weeks not designing anything. Just watching.

Before touching Figma, I needed to understand the actual work — what decisions analysts make, in what order, with what information, and where they fail. Desk research on KYC regulation was table stakes. The real insight came from sitting next to analysts while they worked.

01
Contextual Inquiry
Shadowed 6 KYC analysts across 3 client organisations over 8 working days. Watched how they navigated the existing tool, what they ignored, where they paused, what made them curse under their breath.
02
Stakeholder Interviews
14 interviews — 8 analysts, 3 compliance managers, 2 team leads, 1 auditor. The auditor interview was the most revealing: I needed to understand what evidence they'd look for if something went wrong.
03
Workflow Audit
Mapped every step of the existing review workflow — from case assignment to final decision — including all the workarounds analysts had invented. Each workaround was a signal of an unmet need.
04
Regulatory Research
Read the relevant FATF guidelines, PML Act requirements, and client-specific compliance policies. Designers who build compliance products without reading the regulation are guessing. I didn't want to guess.

02 · Synthesis

Five things that changed everything.

The research produced 60+ observations. After affinity mapping and two rounds of synthesis, these five insights kept surfacing — and each one had direct design consequences.

01
The queue is the wrong unit of organisation.
Analysts received cases in chronological order — first in, first reviewed. But risk doesn't respect chronology. A low-risk case filed an hour ago shouldn't be reviewed before a high-risk case filed yesterday. The queue created a false sense of urgency around the wrong cases.
02
The most dangerous moment is when you think you're done.
Analysts made most errors not when confused, but when confident. After reviewing 40 cases, pattern recognition kicks in — and anomalies get glossed over. There was no mechanism in the UI to slow down decision-making when data was incomplete or contradictory.
03
Context about the customer was being stored in people's heads.
If an analyst had reviewed the same customer's earlier application, that knowledge lived in their memory — not the system. Analysts were using personal spreadsheets to track "known names." This was both an efficiency problem and a compliance risk.
04
The audit trail was retrospective, not concurrent.
Analysts completed a review, then logged their reasoning separately. This meant reasoning was reconstructed post-hoc, not captured in the moment. The audit trail was technically present but evidentially weak — a problem auditors had flagged.
05
Tool-switching was the biggest invisible cost.
Analysts averaged 4.2 browser tabs open simultaneously — the platform, a sanctions list tool, a risk policy doc, a team chat, and often a spreadsheet. Each switch was a cognitive reset. Integrating these touchpoints wasn't feasible, but reducing the need to leave was.
"After 50 cases you stop seeing the customer. You start seeing a pattern. The UI was making that worse."
— KYC Analyst, observed during shadowing session

03 · Define

Reframing the problem as opportunity.

Once I had the insights, I needed design opportunities — not solutions. These HMW statements became the filter every design decision was tested against throughout the project.

HMW surface the highest-risk cases before an analyst even starts their day — without adding a separate triage step?
HMW create natural friction at the moment of decision — not as obstruction, but as a signal that more attention is needed?
HMW make the customer's full history visible at the point of review without adding a navigation step?
HMW capture the analyst's reasoning during the review — not after — so the audit trail is a byproduct of good workflow, not an additional task?
HMW reduce context switching without requiring costly third-party integrations?

04 · Ideation

The concepts I explored — and why most of them were wrong.

I ran two ideation sprints. The first generated 30+ concepts, some of which were technically interesting but behaviourally naive — they assumed analysts would change habits they'd spent years building.


The second sprint was more constrained: every concept had to work within 3 clicks of the existing mental model. The breakthrough ideas came from that constraint.


I tested lo-fi prototypes with 4 analysts in 45-minute sessions. The sessions were as much about watching hesitation and confusion as they were about collecting verbal feedback.

× Gamification layer (added cognitive load, felt patronising)
× Mandatory pause-and-confirm dialogs (analysts closed them immediately)
× Fully AI-driven case routing (compliance managers wouldn't trust it)
✓ Risk-tiered queue with visual weight differentiation
✓ Persistent context panel (always-on, never a modal)
✓ Inline decision annotation (reasoning captured mid-flow)

04b · Design Flow

The redesigned analyst workflow.

The new workflow reduced the number of decision points per case from 7 to 4, without removing any compliance-required checks. Fewer steps, same regulatory rigour.

01
Triage View
Cases sorted by risk tier automatically. High-risk cases surfaced at the top. Analysts start the day with clarity, not chronology.
02
Case Open
Split-pane layout: documents left, decision tools right. Customer history always visible. Zero navigation needed.
03
Review + Annotate
Inline annotation tied to each document section. Reasoning captured as the analyst reviews — not after. Completeness indicator live.
04
Decision
Approve, reject, or escalate — with a required reason code. Soft warning if data completeness is below threshold. A signal, not a blocker.

05 · Design Decisions

Four decisions that defined the product.

Every design has a hundred small decisions. These four had the most argument, the most testing, and the most impact.

Decision 01
Risk tiers, not priority scores
The tension
Engineering wanted a single numerical risk score (0–100). Compliance managers wanted tiered categories. I sided with compliance — numbers create false precision in a domain where context matters. A case that scores 72 is not inherently safer than one that scores 74.
Why it mattered
Tiers (High / Medium / Low / Pending) created clear mental buckets for analysts. They could make a quick judgement about how much attention to bring to a case before opening it. The numerical score remained accessible, but it wasn't the primary signal.
Decision 02
Persistent sidebar vs. expandable panel
The tension
Early testing showed analysts wanted customer history available but not always visible. An expandable panel felt right. But testing with real cases revealed: analysts who had to open the panel, didn't. Out of sight meant out of mind.
Why it mattered
I kept the sidebar persistent but collapsible to a thin rail. The rail showed just the customer's risk history as coloured dots — enough context to prompt expansion when needed, without eating screen real estate when it wasn't.
Decision 03
Soft warning on incomplete decisions
The tension
There was pressure to block decision submission if the completeness score was below 80%. Compliance managers liked the idea. Analysts hated it. Hard blocks breed workarounds — analysts would start marking fields arbitrarily just to unlock the button.
Why it mattered
Instead: a soft warning that names exactly which data points are incomplete, with a prompt to add a justification note. The decision can still be submitted — but the act of writing the justification makes the incompleteness explicit in the audit trail.
Decision 04
Audit trail as workflow, not task
The tension
The existing system required analysts to write a review summary after each case. It was done badly or not at all. I wanted to eliminate this step — but compliance managers couldn't operate without the audit log. The audit trail had to exist. It just couldn't feel like admin.
Why it mattered
Every annotation made during review — each flag, each comment, each reason code — was automatically compiled into the audit log. By the time the analyst submitted, the trail was already written. The summary existed; the analyst just didn't have to write it.

08 · Reflection

What I learned. What I'd do differently.

What worked
The decision to frame the audit trail as a workflow output rather than a task was the single most impactful design move on the project. It took the longest to convince stakeholders but had the most measurable effect. When friction reduction and compliance rigour pointed in the same direction, that's the right design.
What I'd do differently
I would involve the compliance auditor earlier — ideally in the research phase. I only interviewed one auditor, late in discovery. Their perspective on what "good evidence" looks like would have shaped some decisions I made by assumption rather than research.
The uncomfortable truth
The biggest design challenge was not the interface. It was analyst fatigue — a structural, organisational problem that no UI can fix. The redesign improved the tool. It didn't solve the problem of asking people to make high-stakes decisions 60+ times a day. I made that observation in my handoff documentation.
Domain lesson
In regulated industries, design is evidence. The choices you make in the interface become part of the compliance record. This changes how you think about defaults, warnings, confirmations. Every "is this really necessary?" question has a different answer when the answer could end up in a regulator's report.
